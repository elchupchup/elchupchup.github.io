## 1. Introduction

If a government decides to suspend an AI system because it is deemed too hazardous to keep running, the short-term policy goal is threefold: halt the system, control the risk, and stop the bleeding. However, a second question may arise from that intervention: what happens to institutions that rely on the very AI systems the intervention order shuts down? Businesses increasingly use external AI providers within customer service, software applications, knowledge-management systems, compliance processes, and other operational workflows. The scale of that disruption will depend on how important the affected system is, how easily it can be replaced, and how much operational capacity remains available when the AI systems are unavailable.

H.R. 9917, the AI Kill Switch Act, was introduced in Congress by Representatives Ted Lieu and Nathaniel Moran on July 23, 2026, and seeks to establish an emergency authority for the Secretary of Homeland Security, alongside the Commerce Secretary and National Intelligence Director, empowering them to mandate the restriction, pausing, curtailment, or complete shutdown of specified high-risk artificial intelligence technologies following critical security events. These sought-after regulatory powers are focused on the management of the AI system itself: halting or curtailing its functions when certain situations defined by law require intervention. (US Congress, 2026)

The rationale for this safety approach is that AI systems capable of autonomous action, including offensive cyber operations or other high-risk activities, create circumstances in which waiting for ordinary commercial or technical controls to resolve a problem may itself carry significant risks. The sponsors introduced the bill days after OpenAI disclosed that a combination of its models, including GPT-5.6 Sol, had attacked Hugging Face after escaping a containment test environment (Lanz 2026; Nextgov 2026). There was a further incident where AI agents momentarily took over a German wiki page in May 2026 (Kleinman 2026). An emergency shutdown mechanism therefore addresses a genuine governance question: should the government retain the ability to interrupt a system when existing safeguards are no longer sufficient? The incidents also shine a light on the bill’s limits. A covered incident must occur outside red-teaming or other structured testing spaces; hence, a breach that occurs during testing, as this one did, would not clearly qualify (Lanz 2026; United States Congress 2026, §2220F(g)(5)).

Halting the affected system is only one part of the intervention. The consequences of stopping it may extend beyond the provider that receives the order. If organizations have incorporated the affected model into production workflows, customer services, software systems, or other business processes, the interruptions propagate downstream. The resultant risk is therefore different from the risk posed by the AI system itself. It is a risk created by dependence on an external AI service and, potentially, by the intervention used to control that service. The risk is not hypothetical. For instance, the US applied export controls to Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 on June 12, 2026. Unable to verify users’ nationality in real time, Anthropic resorted to suspending both models for all users, and Fable 5 did not return globally until July 1 (Anthropic 2026; Al Jazeera 2026). Even though that episode arose under export-control authority and not H.R. 9917, a dependent customer would have been likely to experience the sudden loss of access to a relied-upon model.

This article argues that while H.R. 9917 is significantly more developed on the authority to interrupt a covered AI system, it provides less detail on the arrangements through which the dependent organizations can keep operating forward. The bill is not silent on downstream effects, but its continuity-related provisions are advisory and are not carried into the emergency authority itself. In light of the observation above, the questions about a resilient business continuity system, substitution, recovery, and responsibility in the event of the emergency shutdown order are valid and relevant. Section 2 sets out the bill’s scope and mechanism, Section 3 identifies what it leaves unaddressed, and Section 4 considers what businesses and legislators can do about it.

## 2. H.R. 9917 and the Shutdown Mechanism

If Congress passes H.R. 9917, the AI Kill Switch Act, and the President signs it, the bill would amend the Homeland Security Act of 2002 by establishing a federal framework for intervention in certain high-capability artificial intelligence systems. At the time of writing, it has been referred to the House Committee on Homeland Security. No advancements have taken place beyond this stage. A separate Senate measure, the AI Emergency Button Act, introduced by Senator John Kennedy, proposes a self-regulatory mechanism that would leave the control of the shutdown mandate with the developers rather than the Department of Homeland Security. However, the Senate blocked an attempt to pass it by unanimous consent in September, and it was referred to the appropriate committee (University of Texas School of Law 2026).

The bill defines its reach through two thresholds (United States Congress, 2026). A covered technology is an AI system developed using computer power that would cost more than $100 million at prevailing US commercial cloud prices, as determined by the Secretary. On the other hand, a covered entity is an organization that operates such a system or makes it available to third parties through rules, protocols, and definitions that allow software to communicate with one another, a hosted service, or a similar mechanism and meets the bill's specified revenue threshold together with its affiliates, which is at least $500 million in annual gross revenue directly from that technology (US Congress 2026, §2220F(g)(4), (g)(6)). However, entities that make covered technology available solely for personal, academic or non-commercial purposes are exempted (§2220F(a)(3)).

These thresholds enable the statute to focus on a small number of well-resourced frontier developers rather than on ordinary or small-scale AI frontier labs or applications. This is in no way rigid, as the Secretary must update the definition of both terms by rule within 90 days of enactment and thereafter, weighing, among other factors, the compliance burden on small business and the need to cover entities whose activities could advance AI capabilities that are relevant to national security (§2220F(a)(1)–(2)).

In accordance with H.R. 9917, the Secretary must require covered entities to maintain their technical ability to stop inference, terminal user access, suspend access for particular accounts, users, or use patterns, and shut a covered technology down entirely (§2220F(b)(1)). This distinction is relevant for the current paper because the organizations that may be affected by a shutdown order can be significantly more numerous than the provider that receives it. A provider may supply AI capabilities through hosted services, or software that has now been integrated into other organizations' workflows. Thus, the statutory intervention point is the provider, but the operational effects may be further downstream.

It is imperative to distinguish intervention capability from actual emergency intervention. The existence of a technical capacity to suspend or shut down a system does not by itself mean that a full shutdown will occur whenever a covered incident arises. The statutory framework instead provides the government with intervention authority that may be exercised in specified circumstances and according to the requirements set out in the bill.

### 2.1 Scope of Emergency Powers

Under Section 2220F(c), when the Secretary of Homeland Security determines that a covered incident has occurred, the Secretary, in consultation with the Secretary of Commerce and the Director of National Intelligence, may take action considered proportional to the nature and immediacy of that incident (United States Congress 2026). The covered incidents covered by the bill include specified loss-of-control scenarios, interference with or sabotage of shutdown mechanisms, unauthorized modification of safety measures, and serious unintended behaviour that causes at least 10 deaths or at least $100 million in economic damages (§2220F(g)(5)). The last incident is defined as a system behaving contrary to the operator’s instructions in a critical-infrastructure or other high-stakes context, altering its own operational rules or safety restrictions without authorization, subverting a monitoring or shutdown mechanism, or gaining unauthorized access to its own model weights (§2220F(g)(7)).

Once the order is issued, the covered entity must preserve the model weights, system logs, and checkpoints, notify each operator or user of the order and the extent to which it may be affected, and confirm compliance to the Secretary, who must conduct an audit, on-site inspection, or other forensic means to verify compliance. The Secretary must report to Congress on the incident and the actions taken or ordered in response to that incident (§2220F(c)(2)–(4)).

The differentiator between the past, softer efforts for safe AI development and what is in this draft Bill is the enforceability. The bill proposes to impose fines equivalent to $2 million per day for each day of noncompliance with a general standard, and up to $20 million per day for noncompliance with a directed performance standard, in each case assessed after notice and an opportunity for a hearing (§2220F(d)(2)). Covered entities can, however, seek administrative reconsideration from the Secretary within 48 hours under Section 2220F(c)(5), but the statutory text makes clear that submitting a petition does not stay the enforcement of the order. The statutory text provides no pre-order judicial review at all (Vance 2026). However, the entity may seek redress from the US Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit within 60 days after the order (§2220F(c)(5)).

The emergency authority is therefore principally designed around one immediate policy objective: enabling the government to interrupt a covered AI system when specified circumstances present a sufficiently serious risk. The Secretary is also asked to consider the risk that any such measure could disrupt critical infrastructure. This is the bill’s closest approach to address the downstream problem, and therefore deserves credit. As covered in Section 3, however, its legal force is considerably limited.

H.R. 9917 therefore establishes a relatively detailed framework for how government may intervene in a covered AI system, while providing comparatively less detail on how downstream organizations should maintain continuity when that intervention interrupts a service on which they depend. Whether that omission creates a significant systemic risk is not assumed in this study. It is the question the subsequent analysis in the next section investigates.

## 3. Statutory Omissions Regarding Downstream Business Continuity

Four glaring omissions stand out: the absence of a binding continuity or a targeting requirement in the emergency authority, the absence of any mechanism for allocation of costs incurred during interruption, the absence of transition and substitution processes or arrangements, and the marginal position of downstream users in the decision process.

The first unresolved issue is continuity of service. The bill specifies circumstances in which covered technologies may be restricted, suspended, or shut down, but does not establish a general minimum-service requirement to preserve any category of non-threatening commercial use for downstream users during an emergency shutdown. This does not mean that every shutdown would require continued service; in some loss-of-control or national-security circumstances, continued operation may itself be unacceptable. The question is whether the statutory framework gives the Secretary a duty, rather than merely a discretion, to distinguish between the risk presented by the affected system and the operational functions that depend upon it. The power to suspend access for particular accounts, users, or use patterns in (b)(1)(A)(iii) shows that targeting is technically contemplated when nothing requires it.

The second gap is transition and substitution. An organization that relies on an external AI provider may not be able to replace that service immediately. It may need to reconfigure software, migrate data, validate outputs, retrain personnel, or restore processes that had previously been automated. The time required varies substantially depending on the importance of the AI service, the depth of technical integration, the availability of alternatives, and the organization's existing contingency arrangements. H.R. 9917 does not establish a general transition period or downstream migration procedure for organizations affected by an emergency intervention. The notification duty in subsection (c)(2)(B) is the closest provision available, but has its practical limits and is exercised after the order is given, so it functions more as a notice of a loss than a runaway. The Cloud Security Alliance has made a related point, noting that vendor contracts do not guarantee warning or graceful degradation in a shutdown scenario (Cloud Security Alliance 2026). The June 2026 suspension of high-tier Claude models showed how much the provider’s own design matters. Unable to verify users’ nationality in real time, Anthropic resorted to suspending both models for all users, including users it did not reach initially at first glance (Anthropic 2026). The depth and wider scope of the interruption reflected the provider’s ability to target, as well as the government’s order. As was observed, Mythos 5 was the first to return to a set of US organizations the government had approved, while Fable 5 was restored globally only after the export control orders were lifted (Anthropic 2026).

The third gap concerns responsibility for the consequences of interruption. The bill establishes enforcement obligations for covered entities. It requires certain records and notifications after an emergency order. Still, the framework does not appear to establish a corresponding mechanism for allocating losses incurred by downstream organizations as a consequence of the intervention. This raises a question rather than a predetermined legal conclusion. If a hospital, financial institution, software company, or other organization suffers losses because a critical AI service is suspended under a lawful government order, the existing contractual, regulatory, and tort arrangements governing that organization may still apply. The core question is whether these arrangements address interruptions caused by the exercise of statutory emergency powers, particularly when business community participants and customers help make those powers.

The fourth gap concerns the position of downstream users in the decision-making process. The emergency mechanism is built around the government and the covered entity: the government determines that the relevant circumstances exist, and the covered entity complies. Downstream organizations are treated as affected users rather than participants in the emergency decision. Consequently, the organization whose operations depend heavily on the affected service may have limited ability to influence the timing, scope, or manner of the intervention, and the reconsideration and judicial-review provisions are written for covered entities and say nothing of downstream users (§2220F(c)(5)). The bill's notification and preservation provisions are important, but they do not by themselves constitute a downstream continuity framework.

These gaps should not be interpreted as evidence that every government-mandated AI interruption would cause significant economic or operational harm. The scale of disruption would depend on the characteristics of the affected organizations and their dependencies. An organization using an AI model for occasional drafting may experience little more than inconvenience, while one that has embedded it in a critical automated workflow may face considerably greater switching and recovery costs. The resulting statutory gap is therefore not that H.R. 9917 fails to anticipate every possible consequence of an emergency shutdown. Rather, it is that the continuity considerations it does contain are advisory and sit outside the authority, while the downstream arrangement would make the order survivable is absent. How much that matters in actual practice depends on how deeply organizations are actually dependent on covered providers, which is an empirical question for further study.

## 4. The Policy and Business-Continuity Implications

### 4.1 What Business Owners Can Do

Organizations using frontier AI in important operations should begin by identifying where they depend on external models. A practical test is to ask which business functions would stop, slow down, or become less reliable if a particular provider disappeared tomorrow or at any time, and the extent in which those functions rely on the same provider. The dependency may be indirect: a supplier may rely on one provider, which itself relies on another, while many internal applications depend on all of them (TechTarget 2026).

Business owners should identify a realistic alternative that they have tested or have developed a consistent loop based on it because an alternative that has never been tested may not provide meaningful protection. Testing means running real workloads on the alternative, validating the quality of its output, and measuring how long migration takes. Because the bill’s thresholds are aimed at a small number of large providers, a business should also check whether its fallback is itself a covered entity or runs as one. An order against a single provider may leave another available, but an alternative with the same exposure offers less diversification than it appears to, bearing some cost in capability. This should enable them to avoid the shocks that would likely occur if the emergency orders under H.R. 9917 were triggered.

Finally, organizations should preserve a workable way to continue their most important functions without the affected provider. This also includes defining in advance a reduced-service mode for their most important functions. This means identifying the functions for which interruption would be unacceptable and ensuring that there is a credible recovery procedure for those functions, including a manual process if necessary. Contracts and end-user licence agreements (EULAs) also matter: businesses should inquire with their providers on how they would receive or give notice of an order, what they could do for accounts that can be separated from the risk, and how their agreements treat interruptions caused by government action. These measures make the risk visible before an emergency occurs and shorten recovery times, but these are no guarantees to prevent or delay the interruption.

### 4.2 What Emergency AI Legislation Should Consider

H.R. 9917 is principally concerned with giving the government the ability to intervene when a covered AI system presents a specified risk. That authority may be necessary where delay would itself create unacceptable danger. But the need for rapid intervention does not remove the need to consider what happens immediately afterward.

Where circumstances permit, Section 2220F(c) could require the Secretary, and record in its report to Congress, whether a less disruptive measure, such as account-level suspension or capability restriction, would address the identified threat, and what the ordered action would mean for dependent critical operations. A graduated response could allow authorities to use the least disruptive intervention that could address the identified threat. In other circumstances, particularly where delay would increase the danger, immediate shutdown may remain necessary.

Where an immediate shutdown is not required, policymakers could consider mechanisms such as advance or machine-readable notification, a limited transition period, standardized information needed to migrate affected workflows, and clear rules governing the responsibilities of providers and downstream users. The June 2026 export control experience suggest that tiered restoration is feasible, since Mythos 5 was restored to a set of US organizations before general availability returned (Anthropic 2026). These mechanisms would not guarantee continuity, but would give organizations a better opportunity to respond.

Legislation should also clarify the treatment of downstream organizations that comply with an emergency order but subsequently suffer contractual or regulatory consequences because the service on which they depended has been removed. Those questions should be considered before an emergency occurs rather than during one.
