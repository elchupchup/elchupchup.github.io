When asking Google a question the first thing that appears is no longer a list of links but an AI-generated summary answering your query. This article seeks to examine the most consequential risks associated with mass integration of AI Overviews (AIOs) into Google's search engine and highlights how it transforms Google's original function as a search engine to a mediator of knowledge. This structural shift replaces human-performed source acquisition, evaluation, and analysis with AI-generated summaries, degrading the quality of public knowledge through systemic misattribution and misrepresentation of claims to their respective sources.[^xu] Google's search engine is not only a for-profit service but has become an essential piece of public infrastructure as the most widely used search engine, controlling about 90% of the global search market.[^murray] Google's shift to generating AI Overviews in response to queries, appearing above the traditional search engine results page (SERP), significantly alters the way users interact with sources of information digitally. Understanding this technology is essential for users to be able to utilize it critically and carefully, especially in the areas in which this technology can be misleading or even harmful. This article highlights specific areas such as health and politics in which AI-generated overviews should be used with extreme caution, and emphasizes the potential concerns regarding the mass integration of AIOs into Google's search engine.

It is important to keep in mind when using this technology that not all generative AI features work the same way even when they are developed by the same company. Firstly, a brief discussion of the differences between results generated by Google's AIOs, Google's chatbot Gemini, and Google's search engine result page will be useful. Surprisingly, the sources retrieved by Gemini and Google's AIOs are quite different and generally inconsistent when faced with minor changes to the query or device type. In fact, out of the sources retrieved by each search method, AIOs and Gemini differ most.[^grossman] Furthermore, Google's AIOs tend to favour sources owned by Google (i.e., google.com and youtube.com domains).[^grossman] This contrasts with Google's SERPs, which are more likely to cite government or educational websites.[^grossman] This highlights that AIOs use a distinct source selection mechanism from the traditional search result page that people are used to.[^xu] These differences in sources retrieved by each method of search may not seem particularly consequential since users can still access the sources that the information is retrieved from. But even slight changes in such widely used digital platforms can cause unintended or unexpected consequences.

One of the most concerning effects of these changes concerns the AIOs' accuracy to the sources they cite and misinformation that is common in response to 'high stakes' queries that can result from this. It is essential to note that the reliability of the sources that appear in AIOs is independent from the overview's accuracy in its representation of the source. When researchers analysed 98,020 claims made in AIOs, 11% of them were found to be unsupported by the sources that were cited.[^xu] Therefore, even if the sources cited are reliable, the claims generated in AIOs are not always faithful to them. This inaccuracy is most consequential when in response to 'high stakes' or sensitive queries such as political, highly contested, or health-related information which are cases in which AIOs commonly appear. In one study, AIOs appeared in response to 93.8% of queries about individual U.S. politicians and, in a separate set of questions, took a negative or affirmative stance 33.4% of the time. By comparison Gemini only took a stance 5.6% of the time in response to the same questions.[^grossman] Additionally, generative AI has been known to 'hallucinate' information, which at times can be attributed to sources that contain no such information.[^xu] In the context of AI, 'hallucination' refers to the tendency of AI models to occasionally output fabricated information that is not linked to (or accurate) to an outside source.

The invasiveness of this AIO feature is also worth emphasizing here; before even having a chance to browse through websites to read from, a summary of the topic, which may take a stance (and may be inaccurate) will be the first thing you read about the topic unless you intentionally scroll past it. Furthermore, it has been found that when AIOs are present, users are far less likely to click on any other websites available on the search result page.[^weinbrand] AIOs present a conclusion before users are able to examine underlying sources, which may encourage users to accept that conclusion without confirming the evidence. This format of web search is not conducive to intentional evaluation of information available on the internet, and instead exposes you first to a synopsis that lacks the nuance and friction of human-written analyses.

Another area where accuracy of AI-generated content is particularly concerning is in response to searches regarding medical or health-related advice. Health-related queries have significant AIO activation rates so the risks here are worth discussing.[^xu] Some professionals in the healthcare industry have expressed concern with inaccurate or misleading health-related information generated by Google's AIOs that could put people in danger. In some cases AIOs have been reported to provide incorrect and misleading advice regarding lifestyle management for pancreatic cancer, advising users to avoid high-fat foods. This suggestion is incorrect as some fats are very important as they are protective against pancreatic cancer—the risk lies in which type of fat you are consuming, not how much.[^jurich] In other cases AIOs were shown to provide misleading or unclear information regarding blood test results, which could discourage users from seeking out necessary treatment.[^gregory] Considering the concerns outlined regarding source choice, claim accuracy, inconsistency, and hallucinations, users should exercise caution when using AIOs for health-related information. AIOs attempt to offer concrete answers and solutions in response to queries, even when there may not be one, which users should keep in mind when they are seeking advice regarding sensitive situations.[^grossman]

Ultimately, the shift towards dependence on AIOs instead of freely browsing Google's search result page not only poses very tangible risks when utilized for health-related advice, but also poses more covert risks in how we evaluate sources and come to conclusions based on AI-generated information. As previously discussed, Google's AI-generated overviews are not neutral but prioritize convenience, simplicity, and immediacy, often even taking a stance in response to controversial queries. This could shift who and what is treated as a trusted source of information since users no longer bear the responsibility of choosing and evaluating sources independently. Now, not only is the choice of sources outsourced, but the analysis of those sources is distilled into a simplified synopsis devoid of uncertainty, which may ultimately undermine agency and intelligence. This brings into question Google's role as not only a for-profit company, but also as the most widely used search engine. As Weinbrand and colleagues note, AIOs fundamentally alter the relationship between users, content creators, and platforms by synthesizing information from multiple sources and generating a curated output which inherently alters the flow of knowledge and the placement of epistemic authority.[^weinbrand] This raises the question: when companies place software in a position of epistemic authority, who is responsible when such software generates incorrect, misleading, or biased responses that have real-world implications? People have different perspectives on this issue, but importantly there is no legal consensus regarding who should bear the consequences when shortcomings in this technology lead to tangible harm.

Until progress is made on the problem of who is responsible for harmful outputs generated by AIOs (and AI in general), the path forward will be difficult. Lack of legal responsibility discourages careful implementation of this technology and leaves many users who are dependent on Google's search engine at risk. Furthermore, the mass integration of AI-generated content into essential infrastructure places users in the role of passive recipients of curated information rather than active participants in their search for knowledge. However, just because AIOs are now placed above the traditional search engine result page does not mean that users must passively accept this as the new standard. If the risks of this feature are emphasized, users may choose to accept AIOs with caution or seek to evaluate sources on their own terms. The current implementation of this software does not necessitate users' dependence on it and it does not guarantee an inevitable shift towards further integration of generative AI into everyday platforms. Users do not have to passively accept the encroachment of AI technology into critical infrastructure, such as Google's search engine. Alternative search engines that let users turn off AI-generated answers, such as DuckDuckGo, are an option for those who prefer to avoid AIOs. Additionally, exercising caution when utilizing AI overviews by reviewing all cited sources and verifying all health related claims with your doctor can reduce risks associated with reliance on AIOs. This article seeks to remind individuals of their capacity to evaluate sources on their own terms and encourages them to take ownership in their search for knowledge by being critical and cautious of who or what is filtering that information.
