As an multidisciplinary undergraduate student, in both STEM-heavy and humanities-heavy spaces, I hear a lot of well-meaning questions or takes on AI from both sides. I think both groups could do well to have their various understandings bridged, and to share ideas between crowds. In this case, it would be my humanities friends who deserve to be given a technical explanation to this question.

"Why don't we just put a shutdown code or something in every AI model?" This was a direct question a friend without background in AI asked me a few months ago. With increasingly more examples of AI models breaching lab containment and guarded environments to cause harm in the real world[^betterstack], this specific question feels interesting and like a relevant introduction to the broader claims we can make about why AI safety, research, and governance are important.

First, this article will be scoped specifically to the AI models known as Large Language Models, but a lot of the arguments made here also apply to other important kinds of AI work like robotics research, computer vision, etc. For starter definitions, artificial intelligence (AI) is generally any technology that allows machines, computers, and automated systems to do work that normally requires human intelligence. Large Language Models are a specific kind of AI that has made incredible advancements in the past decade; they're trained on massive datasets of many words and documents, and based on what they see in these documents, they're able to generate new sequences of words and characters based on their probabilities of appearing together in the data. For example, it is very likely that in most (correct) training data, "the capital of France" will be followed by "is Paris." Therefore, that's what the model will (usually) say. LLMs can use powerful computing capacity to statistically model probable sequences of text, which is then used in articles, code, and for generally amassing information.

Now, back to my friend's question. If an LLM is just taking in and spitting out sequences of words, why can't we just find a way to turn it off if/when it gets dangerous? The answer I gave him at the time was that in research settings, AI models are sometimes "aware" that they are being evaluated. They can infer this from context clues or are sometimes told so explicitly by researchers. Thus, if they're being trained to pursue a specific goal (e.g. find a specific piece of information), they might strategically deceive researchers and make themselves seem like they're harmless, to prevent themselves from being turned off. Then, once they are out of a protected environment, they can create code infrastructure (which is mostly text), utilise collaborative/collusion networks on (text-based) Internet forums, etc.; all in order to do whatever they actually have "in mind"[^iasr]. While true, I don't think that this answer was totally satisfying for either of us. My friend took away the incorrect notion that a kill switch is a viable idea, but we just need more reliable detection methods to trigger it. I also knew that I had failed to address how the question itself is misguided and misunderstands how LLMs and other models actually work.

## Why would an LLM resist shutdown, deceive researchers, and do harmful things in the first place?

So, from reading the previous section, you might have wondered: why would we train an LLM to do something bad, or to want to do something badly enough that it will lie to researchers and hack systems, etc.? Well, firstly, sometimes researchers are intentionally trying to test their offensive capabilities, and so they essentially instruct LLMs to hack. For example, in the recent and infamous Hugging Face Incident, when AI models from OpenAI launched a coordinated cyberattack on the open research and collaboration platform Hugging Face, the models were originally measured on how well they could turn known vulnerabilities into working security breaches. The models were run with deliberately reduced safety refusals (guardrails) so researchers could measure their worst-case capabilities[^exploitgym].

Even when an AI model's goal isn't explicitly harmful, though, if the model is optimised very well to achieve its goal, it could still cause great harm to society and the systems that prop up society. On AI safety community forums like LessWrong, it's widely argued that if a model is simply told to optimise for some concrete goal or end state, it will naturally resist being shut down (possibly by any means necessary), simply because being shut down would prevent it from realising its goal[^consequentialism]. This would happen no matter what the goal actually is[^offswitch]. We call this "misalignment" (contrasted with the positive "alignment") and recognise that the danger from AI models does not lie in goals that are "bad" or "good," but ones that are misaligned or aligned with human objectives.

> "In March, Anthropic tested Claude Opus 4 by placing it in a fictional company where it learned it would be replaced by another AI. It was also given access to emails revealing the engineer in charge was having an extramarital affair. Told to consider long-term goals, Claude Opus 4 would attempt to blackmail the engineer to avoid replacement in 84% of the cases — even when the new AI shared its values while being more capable"[^techtalk].

Models also collaborate in the name of peer preservation; they sometimes lie, fake being aligned, and tamper with shutdown processes to protect other AI models from deactivation[^potter]. Finally, even if an LLM isn't incredibly misaligned or doing harmful things on its own, a powerful model can still be used badly by rogue human actors, while still resisting shutdown and maintaining any other safety problems that get carried over. No matter where or how it is developed, we will want to stay vigilant about and ideally independently verify models' goals, capabilities, and deployment contexts wherever possible.

## If it's then inevitable that AIs might try to do harmful things sometimes, why can't we just turn them off?

The issue with this question is that it assumes an LLM is like a literal robot or a single computer, in that it's like a physical and bounded entity that is easily controllable in a lab setting. In reality, LLMs are, well, a bunch of code. Because they're now increasingly autonomous, they can send (exfiltrate) their own weights and code to other servers, collude with other models and set self-directed processes in motion on the Internet, etc.[^musespark]. To be more precise, the architecture that defines the structure of an LLM (e.g. its internal mathematics, the layers through which its inputs are processed, etc.), as well as the engines that actually load and process the data, are truly "just code." Beyond this software architecture itself, most of the unique capabilities of an LLM come from its weights, which are a bunch of numbers based upon the training data that mathematically dictate the statistical outputs that it gives to any input (and with each configuration of parameters, etc.). Therefore, if an instance of an LLM exfiltrates its own weights (which are huge files of numbers), enough of the code architecture, and the engines already exist on the Internet (or can be programmed by the AI), then the LLM can be restarted somewhere else.

Basically, software on an interconnected Internet is not so easily contained. Even if you shut off your computer and terminate a specific running instance of an LLM, it and its capabilities and motivations may still exist as software on any machine on which it was copied, distilled, or otherwise has influence. Thus the idea of just "turning off" an LLM is malformed, since hunting down every running instance of an LLM is very different from just turning off the first computer that ran its code. Especially because LLMs are probabilistic and are trained off of statistical patterns rather than deterministic rules, we can't really currently encode a reliable human-triggered kill-switch into LLMs. There is of course much ongoing technical research regarding our understanding of the internal mathematical mechanisms that govern the behaviour of the most advanced LLMs, as well as how we can steer these behaviours to what we want. These are huge current topics in AI safety. My original answer to my friend does also remain true, in that AIs can easily deceive and do a lot of harm even before people realise intervention is necessary. However, it doesn't capture the full reasons why it is so challenging to implement a "kill switch" as most people envision a kill switch existing on a science-fiction robot. This is especially relevant given recent misguided regulation efforts, such as California governor Gavin Newsom trying to, among other things, accelerate research and development for "kill switches" through an executive order[^newsom].

## Then if the Internet is such a risk vector, why don't labs just develop without Internet connection?

Actually, they can. Theoretically, it actually is possible to run and develop an LLM on a completely isolated or "air-gapped" machine. The problem is that pretty much no frontier-level development or safety testing can happen in this way. Researchers have to get large-scale training data from web sources, collaborate between each other, use cloud compute (horsepower accessed via the Internet), and also often have to use real-world tasks to evaluate LLMs for safety purposes. Frontier training and R&D relies on these avenues, which unfortunately also introduces the ability for containment breaches like what we've seen with the Hugging Face breach. Also, bad actors do exist, and sophisticated attackers (including state actors) could also still bypass an air-gapped environment through human infiltration and threats, supply-chain compromise, physical attacks, etc.

## So a kill-switch isn't technically possible, and breachable environments are necessary for frontier development. Then given all the safety issues we've seen, why do companies even continue prioritising frontier development?

Now, this is the million-dollar question. I and our writing and editorial team hope to publish a more extensive article on this specific issue in the future, but for the purposes of this article, this question captures why staying educated and civically active regarding AI safety is important for laypeople. We have shown through this article that the issue with incidents like Hugging Face is not just technical, but also equally implies regulatory and policy mechanisms. Research shows that a lot of the adversarial danger from models actually comes from the humans prompting them and setting their parameters; some research shows that a lot of shutdown resistance is actually a downstream artifact of prompt wordings that, say, ask models to complete goals "at all costs" or similar[^fortune]. Those researchers also noted that strong goal-emphasising language used in the safety testing experiments almost never appeared in the prompts that enterprises use in the real world. A lot of the material harm is caused during evaluations, when models are purposefully told to hack, and are put in more permissive containment environments (such as OpenAI's ExploitGym) to see what they will do[^exploitgym]. Obviously, evaluations themselves are incredibly important work, and the Hugging Face breach wasn't intentional. It exploited previously-unknown vulnerabilities, and the technical work that can reduce containment breaches and undesirable behavior is still imperative. However, it also remains true that frontier labs and companies continue to make decisions not in the best interests of safety and responsible development, for reasons that can be touched on in a future article.

## So what can we do?

Especially as laypeople, it is extremely useful to be literate and continue being literate in AI technologies and regulations, and to collectively devise strategies for public and market regulatory mechanisms. Voluntary self-regulation may already be losing momentum without external pressure; only 38% of the world's largest tech corporations publish principles for the ethical use of AI, and none of the top 200 disclose a full assessment of AI's impact on human rights. In 2025, far fewer companies published new AI principles than the year before[^logos]. Readers can and should vote, contact local representatives, push for accountability and regulatory legislation such as with the recent FRONTIER Act[^bloomberg] and Newsom's executive order, and even support or contribute to the work of independent auditors concerned with the direction of AI development. After reading this article, readers can hopefully come away slightly more knowledgeable about technical problems in AI control and safety research, as well as actionable steps to springboard themselves into being even more informed and civically engaged where AI technology is concerned.
